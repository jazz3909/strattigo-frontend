Glycolysis and the pathway to pyruvate

How the cell breaks a single glucose molecule into two molecules of pyruvate — and banks a small, fast profit of ATP along the way.

Generated from 6 materials Detailed style Updated today

Glycolysis is the oldest and most universal way that living cells extract energy from sugar. It happens in the cytoplasm, needs no oxygen, and runs in nearly every organism on Earth — which is a strong hint that it evolved very early. Before you memorize the ten steps, it helps to hold the shape of the whole thing in your head: one six-carbon sugar goes in, two three-carbon molecules come out, and the cell nets a little usable energy in the process.

Key idea

Glycolysis is an investment. The cell spends 2 ATP up front to destabilize glucose, then earns 4 ATP back — for a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose.

The two halves of the pathway

Preparatory phase, then payoff phase

The ten reactions divide cleanly into two phases. The first is the preparatory phase, where the cell actually spends energy. It uses 2 ATP to add phosphate groups to glucose, making the molecule less stable and easier to split. This is the "you have to spend money to make money" half.

The second is the payoff phase, where the destabilized fragments are oxidized and the cell collects its return: 4 ATP and 2 NADH. Because two three-carbon molecules travel through this phase, everything in it happens twice per glucose.

Glucose + 2 NAD+ + 2 ADP + 2 Pi  →  2 Pyruvate + 2 NADH + 2 ATP

Why the cell bothers with NADH

The 2 ATP are the obvious prize, but the 2 NADH matter just as much. Each NADH is a loaded electron carrier — under aerobic conditions it will later be cashed in at the electron transport chain for far more ATP. Glycolysis, in other words, is only the opening move.

Remember for the exam
  • Net yield per glucose: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate
  • Location: cytoplasm — no membrane, no oxygen required
  • Regulation happens mainly at phosphofructokinase (step 3)
  • The payoff phase runs twice, because glucose is split in two

Where it's regulated

The committed step

If you only remember one control point, make it phosphofructokinase-1. This enzyme catalyzes the first truly committed step of glycolysis, and it responds to the cell's energy charge: when ATP is abundant, it slows down; when the cell is energy-hungry and AMP is high, it speeds up. This is a textbook example of feedback inhibition and a favorite exam target.
